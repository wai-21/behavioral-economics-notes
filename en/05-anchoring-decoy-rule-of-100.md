# 05. Anchoring & Decoys: How "Was ¥1000, Now ¥300" Makes Your Heart Race

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Erase the historical coordinate; ask only current value vs alternatives.** "Fell from 1000 to 300" and "is it worth 300" are unrelated questions — the first is an anchor problem, the second is the pricing problem.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **The scene: two thermoses, same final price.** Store A: ¥300, "practical, in stock." Store B: "was ¥1000, limited-time 70% off, now ¥300." Identical quality, identical outlay — yet most people's hearts race at B, feeling they'd "gain" and might "miss out."
- **Mechanism 1: anchoring and adjustment.** The first number received becomes the mental anchor; every later judgment is an adjustment from it — **and adjustments are undershooting**. B's "1000" doesn't participate in computation, only in **feeling**.
- **Mechanism 2: the decoy effect (asymmetric dominance).** Cinema popcorn:
  - Only "Small ¥100 / Large ¥190" → many pick Small.
  - Add "Medium ¥180" → Small buyers switch to the ¥190 Large.
  - **Almost nobody buys the ¥180 Medium. It exists only to make ¥190 look cheap** — moving the battlefield from "100 vs 190" to "180 vs 190," manufacturing the "only ¥10 more to upgrade" feeling.
- **Mechanism 3: framing & the Rule of 100.** Same ¥200 saved: under ¥100, "20% off" beats "save ¥10"; over ¥100 (a ¥30,000 laptop), "save ¥6000" beats "20% off." Not rational weighing — the fast-scanning brain is attracted to **the absolute size of numbers**: 6000 shocks more than 20.
- **String them together:** pricing isn't asking you "is it worth it," it's asking "**compared to what**" — and the comparison set is arranged by the seller. "Cheap" is almost always a designed feeling.

> *"The ¥179 medium tub isn't really for sale. It exists purely to show off how cheap the ¥190 large tub is."* (paraphrased; confidence: medium)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** I treated strike-through prices as "reference info" and figured I was immune to "was ¥1000." I just "happened to look."
- **After:** two quiz questions went fine — I could spot anchoring **in someone else's design**. The turn came in the practical-reflection round: recalling my own trapped purchase, I named **Taobao's "add one item while you're here"** — a discounted trinket at the payment page, and somehow cups and trash bags I didn't need kept coming home. The dialogue decomposed it into **a three-bias stack, none of which was "I believed a fake original price"**:
  ① **Anchoring** — a ¥30 cup struck through to "add-on price ¥9.9" makes 9.9 feel dirt cheap;
  ② **Mental accounting** — the main order is already ¥300; "another 9.9 is nothing" — pain near zero;
  ③ **Hot state + System 1** — countdown timer, rush to finish checkout, no time to ask "does my home actually lack trash bags."
- **The third layer:** my old anti-anchor move was "don't trust the strike-through." That's **one third of the countermeasure** — the real hole was treating "the main order is already big" as a reason. The upgrade is **context stripping**: pull the add-on out and ask — **"If this were listed alone on the homepage at ¥9.9, would I click in and buy it?"** Usually no, and the question yanks the trinket out of the big order's mental account, waking System 2.

```text
Anti-anchor + anti-decoy checklist (shopping and procurement)

  [Before entering]
  □ What's my budget? — set it before seeing any price
  □ What problem am I solving? — write the problem, not the product name

  [When seeing a price]
  □ Strike-through / original / reference price → sales copy, not information
  □ Ask: if it was always listed at this price (no comparison), would I buy?
  □ Ask: of the options in front of me, which would almost nobody buy?
     → that's the decoy; it defines "good value"

  [Checkout page — highest-risk zone]
  □ "Add one item" appears → context-strip question:
     "If it were on the homepage alone, would I click in for it?"
  □ Countdown appears → hot-state signal; no irreversible spending on the spot
  □ Ask: is this ¥9.9 being folded into the "already spent a lot" account?
```
