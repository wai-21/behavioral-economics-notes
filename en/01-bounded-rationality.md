# 01. Bounded Rationality: Why You Grab the Toast Instead of Comparison Shopping

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Treat "effort-saving" as a design variable, not a moral flaw.** Fighting your compute budget with willpower loses; moving high-stakes decisions into a pre-written checklist routes around it.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **The "rational agent" of classical economics is a calculator, not a person.** It assumes that facing 20 toasts you'd read every price, weight, calorie and ingredient, compute the optimal nutrition-per-yuan, and even walk to three neighboring supermarkets — then pick the theoretical optimum.
- **Real people satisfice.** After a full workday, tired and hungry, you glance at the package that "looks right," grab it, and go. Not optimal — **good enough**.
- **Herbert Simon named this bounded rationality.** Three hard caps constrain it: ① memory and information processing; ② time to decide; ③ energy. The optimal real-world strategy is therefore **lowering search costs**, not exhausting search.
- **Key corollary: whether a strategy is "good" depends on the environment, not its purity.** In a cheap-information, expensive-time environment, grabbing the familiar toast is the *correct* power-saving move. It fails in exactly one place — when you carry the habit onto phone plans, bank loans and insurance: decisions that are **high-cost and irreversible**.

> *"Behavioural economics brings the real human nature of psychology, neuroscience and sociology into the cold world of economics."* (paraphrased from the source dialogue; confidence: medium)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** I treated comparison shopping as a character trait. Overpaying meant insufficient discipline, so every fix was "try harder next time."
- **After:** asked what happens when the grab-the-familiar habit meets phone plans, loans or insurance, I didn't answer abstractly — I reported a real failure: *I bought a heavily marketed, eye-catching package deal with poor value.* The wake-up: **merchants know my brain wants to save effort, so they invest in packaging, not product.** I had been mistaking eye-catchingness for value. My laziness isn't my defect — it's **their pricing variable**.
- **The third layer:** if effort-saving gets priced, the fix isn't more willpower — it's **moving high-risk decisions from in-the-moment judgment to a pre-written checklist**. In-the-moment judgment always loses to marketing that was computed in advance.

```text
Decision triage (my current version)

  Is this decision reversible?
  ├─ Reversible, low cost (toast, takeout) → effort-saving lane, no guilt
  └─ Irreversible, high cost (plans / loans / insurance / big subscriptions)
       ↓ forced into the checklist lane
       ① Write down the problem I actually need solved (not the product name)
       ② List 2 alternatives (even ones I won't pick)
       ③ Ask: what does this page most want me to look at?
          → usually not price, but packaging, bonuses, countdown timers
       ④ If I can't say in one sentence why it beats the alternatives → don't buy
```
