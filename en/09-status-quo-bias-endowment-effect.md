# 09. Status-Quo Bias & the Endowment Effect: Why Free Trials Refuse to Be Cancelled

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Ask the zero-based question once a day.** "Holding the existing position" and "buying at the current price" carry identical opportunity cost — status-quo bias makes them feel different; on the books they're the same trade.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **The scene: the streaming free trial.** "Free for 30 days, cancel anytime; from day 31, auto-renews at ¥300/month."
  - Everyone plans: "I'll watch free for a month and set an alarm to cancel the day before."
  - Reality: a month passes, the service goes unwatched, and the monthly charge silently continues.
- **A two-punch combo:**
  - 📦 **The endowment effect** — after 30 days of building playlists and watch history, the brain treats the service as **"mine."** Cancelling triggers a strong sense of deprivation and loss aversion. The sharp part: **the trial's real product is not the experience — it's the feeling of ownership.**
  - 🛋️ **Status-quo bias** — "auto-renew" is the **default state**. As long as changing it costs effort, inertia keeps everything as-is and payments flowing. Change requires extra effort and risk; keeping the status quo requires nothing.
- **A third transferable scene:** the job-choice quiz. Current salary ¥1,000,000; a headhunter offers ¥1,050,000 for near-identical work. He stays, reasoning "adapting is risky; I might fail; and I'm used to the team — ¥50k isn't worth it." That's **status-quo bias + loss aversion**: the imagined adaptation setback (a loss) psychologically outweighs the raise (a gain).
- **Generalized:** the endowment effect explains "why owning makes it harder to release"; status-quo bias explains "why the un-done stays un-done." Their shared cure is not "be more rational" — it is **changing the default**.

> *"The platform sets 'auto-renew' as the default. As long as we don't spend effort changing the setting, inertia keeps everything unchanged."* (paraphrased; confidence: medium)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** I treated "not switching" as a neutral, safe option. Switch = active risk; stay = untouched. So my default was "unless the new option is clearly better, don't move."
- **After:** I answered both identification questions correctly — the job-choice one (status-quo bias + loss aversion) and the free-trial one (endowment effect + status-quo bias). **What actually stopped me was the wording of the zero-based rule:**

  > "If I held the equivalent in cash today, would I buy this position at the current price?" If no, reduce or exit immediately.

  Its power: **it redefines "not switching" from "safe" to "an active buy decision."** I'm not choosing between holding and switching — I'm choosing between *buying A at today's price* and *buying B at today's price*. **The option "maintain the status quo" does not exist.**
- **The third layer:** my default got inverted. From "unless clearly better, don't move" to **"unless I can say why it beats the alternative, don't hold it."** The same inversion applies to subscriptions, vendors, jobs, relationships: **renewal is not a default — it's a fresh decision.**

```text
Anti-status-quo execution checklist

  [Financial positions]
  Every session, pre-market: the zero-based question
  → no → reduce or exit at market immediately

  [Subscriptions and paid services]
  Keep a "renewal = repurchase" ledger: monthly fee, last actual use,
  and the alternative for each subscription
  → "last use" more than 30 days ago → cancel, no discussion
  → at free-trial signup, write the cancellation date into the calendar
    immediately (not "remember to cancel")

  [Major choices (jobs / partnerships / plans)]
  Write "keep the status quo" down as an explicit option — and price it
  → ask: what does staying one more year COST (not gain)?
  → then ask: of the new option's risks, how much is budgetable
    adaptation cost?
```
