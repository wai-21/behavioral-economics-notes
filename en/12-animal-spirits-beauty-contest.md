# 12. Animal Spirits: Why History Keeps Minting the Same Bubble

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **A one-sided consensus is a risk alarm.** "Everyone agrees" means every potential short's ammunition has already converted into long positions — when the marginal increment slightly disappoints, there is no one left to buy.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **The scene: from tulip mania to housing bubbles.** 17th-century Holland: a single rare tulip bulb, the *Semper Augustus*, traded at a price that could **buy a mansion in central Amsterdam**.
- **Two worldviews collide:**
  - 📈 **Classical macro** assumes prices precisely reflect true value; everyone is a calmly computing rational investor.
  - 🌊 **The real macro market**: Keynes pointed out that markets run on **animal spirits** and crowd emotion. Seeing neighbors get rich, optimism and greed self-reinforce.
- **Mechanism 1: animal spirits.** The instinct, impulse and optimism that drive entrepreneurs to invest and take risk — **not measurable by any cold formula**. It is the fuel of investment *and* the fuel of bubbles: the same force, in both phases.
- **Mechanism 2: social mood.** Collective emotion **self-realizes**: in sunny seasons traders feel luckier, take more risk, and can move equity markets. **Mood is not a reaction to fundamentals — it is one of the fundamentals.**
- **Mechanism 3 (the sharpest cut): Keynes's beauty contest.** A newspaper prints 100 faces; the prize goes not to the face *you* find prettiest but to the face *you think most people* will pick.
  - So the rational strategy is judging "who others will find pretty," not "who is beautiful."
  - Then: "who others will think *others* will find pretty" — **the recursion never ends**.
  - **Corollary: in speculative markets, the speculator's focus is not intrinsic value but "what price the crowd will shortly believe others will pay."** Markets can stay detached from fundamentals for a long time — that's not a malfunction; it's the game's rules.
- **Generalized:** micro biases are "individual judgment errors"; macro bubbles are **those errors amplified by institutions and leverage**. As Keynes (attributed) put it: markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent — a bubble needs no fundamentals, only a continuing marginal buyer.

> *"Keynes's beauty contest captures the speculator's psychology: caring not about intrinsic value, but about forecasting other people's forecasts."* (paraphrased; confidence: medium)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** I understood bubbles as "a crowd losing its mind," so I figured that staying clear-headed and computing fundamentals made me immune. Bubbles were other people's problem.
- **After:** the quiz asked what speculators care most about, per the beauty contest. I answered correctly — the crowd's forecast of other people's willingness to pay. **But answering it correctly exposed my blind spot:** if the market is a beauty contest, then "I computed the fundamentals" doesn't immunize me — it just means I'm **playing a different game**, holding a different game's scoreboard. I hadn't "seen through the bubble"; I had **declined to enter that game**.
- **The third layer:** the six rules of sentiment module share one logic — **turn "market sentiment" into an observable input**, not a despised phenomenon:

```text
Market sentiment dashboard (six gauges; any extreme → de-risk)

  ① Are non-investor friends recommending an instrument unprompted?
     → yes: herding at peak
  ② Can my buy thesis survive without the last two weeks of headlines?
     → no: availability premium
  ③ Is the gap between price and a DCF model widening?
     → yes, with no new fundamentals: animal spirits
  ④ Mass forced liquidations / indiscriminate selling?
     → yes: capitulation bottom approaching (left-side accumulation condition)
  ⑤ Is only one voice left, with "this time is different" as the mainstream?
     → yes: raise hedge ratio
  ⑥ Am I feeling a strong urge to trade during violent intraday swings?
     → yes: no operations of any kind
```
