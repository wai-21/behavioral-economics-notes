# 02. The Crowding-Out Effect: Why a Fine Made Parents Late More Often

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **What rules and recognition can fix, don't fix with money.** One fine swaps the account type — from "what kind of person am I" to "what does this cost" — and the latter can always be renegotiated.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **The experiment: an Israeli daycare's late-pickup fine.** To curb parents picking up children late, the daycare introduced a monetary penalty.
  - **Classical prediction:** raising the price of lateness → fewer late pickups.
  - **Actual result:** lateness **rose sharply** — and, crucially, **did not fall back after the fine was removed**.
- **The mechanism: extrinsic incentives crowd out intrinsic motivation.** Two motivation systems:
  - 💖 **Intrinsic:** duty, morality, empathy, enjoyment ("I feel bad making the teachers stay late").
  - 💰 **Extrinsic:** money, recognition, rules.
- **Why is it irreversible?** Before the fine, parents read "late" as a **moral problem**. After it, the same act was rewritten as a **market transaction** (buying after-hours childcare). Once rewritten, cancelling the fine doesn't reinstall the moral reading — it just becomes "the service stopped being sold," not "I owe someone again."
- **Generalized: the type of incentive changes the nature of the behavior.** Money works on standardizable, measurable tasks; on tasks that run on duty, identity and creativity, it is often poison.

> *"Once the fine was introduced, parents' reading of 'late' subtly shifted — from guilt toward the teachers to 'I paid for late pickup service.'"* (paraphrased; confidence: medium)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** my default fix was "pay more / fine more." Underperformance? Raise the KPI. Can't stick to something? Deposit scheme. Money was my universal pressure valve.
- **After:** in the quiz I actually defended the parents: **"It's a paid service, clearly priced — not a punitive fee."** The dialogue confirmed that this *is* the mechanism — I wasn't changed by the fine, I was changed by **my own re-pricing story**. The real wake-up: **I will actively invent a rationalization that downgrades a moral problem into a transactional one.**
- **The third layer:** crowding-out has a hidden symmetric use — **it can also dissolve psychological burdens**. That's why "I paid for it" makes queue-jumping, no-shows and procrastination guilt-free. The skill: when I hear myself say "I paid anyway," pause and ask — am I **buying a service**, or **buying an exemption from guilt**?

```text
Incentive design self-check (works on yourself and others)

  What kind of behavior am I trying to improve?
  ├─ Standardizable, measurable, boring (data entry) → money works, use it
  └─ Runs on duty, identity, meaning, creativity (care, writing, research, family)
       ↓ before adding money, ask three questions
       ① Is this currently a moral/identity matter or a transaction?
          → already a transaction: fine. A moral one: money swaps the account
       ② If I remove the money later, will behavior be worse than before?
          → "yes" means you bought a short-term rental, not lasting change
       ③ Is there a no-money alternative? Reduce friction, set the right default
          → change defaults, not prices — the highest-ROI intervention
```
