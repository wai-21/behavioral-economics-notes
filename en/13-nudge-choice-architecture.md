# 13. Nudges: How the Default Decides Whether You Donate Organs

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Design your own defaults.** Not with resolve — by setting the good option to "what happens if you do nothing." Resolve is a consumable; defaults are equipment.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **The scene: the organ-donation registration form.** Two countries, identical cultures and campaigns, radically different consent rates:
  - 📝 **Country A (opt-in)**: the form defaults to "no donation"; donors must actively tick a box.
  - 📝 **Country B (opt-out)**: the form defaults to "donation"; non-donors must actively tick a box.
  **Consent rates differ by multiples.**
- **The mechanism: nudges and default options.** The definition rests on **three "no"s**: no option is banned, no economic incentive is significantly changed, nothing is forced — only the **choice architecture** changes.
- **Why defaults are so powerful: they absorb every mechanism from the earlier chapters.**
  - **Status-quo bias** (doing nothing is free) → almost nobody changes the setting;
  - **Bounded rationality** (save compute) → the default is perceived as a pre-vetted recommendation, not re-evaluated;
  - **Loss aversion & the endowment effect** → switching from "already agreed" to "not agreed" is an active act of deprivation — psychologically expensive.
- **The boundary, precisely drawn:** a nudge is **not**:
  - ❌ mandates or legal penalties (that's coercion);
  - ❌ malicious dark patterns (they betray the welfare-improving intent).
  ✅ A nudge = **all options retained + default changed + exit always available**. The opt-out auto-enrolled pension plan (Save More Tomorrow) is the canonical positive case: using status-quo bias to lead people into saving for their future.
- **Generalized:** chapter 9 closes the book — **if biases are predictable, they can be designed for.** The same machinery harvests for merchants, benefits for policy, and disciplines your future self. The difference isn't the mechanism; it's **who designs the default**.

> *"By setting the default option and leveraging status-quo bias, employees are naturally led to save for their future."* (paraphrased; attribution to Thaler & Sunstein — confidence: high)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** I filed nudges under "public policy" — a tool for governments and platforms. For me it was knowledge, not method.
- **After:** two pieces of material turned it into my own tool. First, the quiz: which design best fits the nudge philosophy? — the auto-enrolled retirement plan **with free opt-out at any time**. Correct; A and D are coercion, B is a dark pattern. Second, and more important, the source made me **run a full personal nudge design** on my own pain point — reducing late nights, triggered by short-video scrolling. It refused to hand me a tips list and instead **forced the design to completion one question at a time**:

| Step | My answer | The mechanism it added |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Physical isolation (raise the cost) | Charge the phone in the living room overnight | Let the cold state build friction for the hot state |
| Remove the escape hatch | My excuse: "I need the phone as my alarm" | "Eliminate the exception" or the isolation gets revoked |
| Choose the concrete plan | Phone stays the alarm — in the farthest room, at max volume | Physical distance raises the cost of the first move |
| If-Then trigger | "After brushing my teeth" → phone to the dining table | Habit stacking; zero memory load |
| Morning patch | After silencing the alarm → open the curtains, get sunlight | Light suppresses melatonin, raises cortisol, wakes System 2 |

  **The complete loop:** 🪥 evening trigger — after brushing teeth, phone goes to the dining table charging with a max-volume alarm; ⏰ morning response — walk to the table to silence the alarm → eyes immediately go to the window, curtains open, sunlight in.
- **The third layer:** this section changed nudge's status entirely. It's not a piece of knowledge — it's **the only framework that converts every previous mechanism into a positive tool**. The logic is simple: every bias says "environment beats willpower," so run it in reverse — **don't change yourself; change the environment.** That's also why this archive ends with "turn 24 rules into an agent filter": that is **handing my defaults to a process that never gets tired.**

```text
Personal nudge design template (a blueprint, not advice)

  Step 1 · Classify the problem
    Is this a time-inconsistency problem? (daytime intentions ≠ present wants)
    → yes → precommitment; no → friction adjustment

  Step 2 · Find the trigger
    Attach to an action that already happens daily
    (brushing teeth / payday / clocking out / market close)
    → test: it must fire without reminders

  Step 3 · Lower the friction of good / raise the friction of bad
    ✅ Good: equipment staged, process on the wall, environment pre-set
    🚫 Bad: physical distance, extra steps, passwords not in my hand

  Step 4 · Kill the exception excuses (most-skipped step)
    Pre-eliminate every "so I can't do it today"
    e.g. "I need the phone as my alarm" → a separate alarm clock,
    or the phone at maximum distance + maximum volume

  Step 5 · Patch the sewer (find the weakest link)
    Ask: which step will break first?
    → that step gets a no-decision-required action
      (e.g. curtains open immediately after silencing the alarm)
```
