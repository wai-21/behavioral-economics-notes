# 04. Availability: Why You Won't Fly After Watching Crash News

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Discount any recently high-frequency information before it enters a judgment.** Reporting density is a lagging indicator — it reflects price moves that already happened, not expected returns.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **Heuristics** are the brain's ready-made shortcut rules, deployed to save compute. Most of the time they're right — the saved compute is itself a gain.
- **The availability heuristic:** when estimating how likely something is, we rely on **how easily it is retrieved from memory**.
  - A fresh, vivid plane-crash story → subjective probability balloons → months of avoiding flights in favor of long drives (statistically far riskier).
  - Passwords set to "123456" or "password" — because those strings surface first.
  - Never switching telecom providers — familiar experience is the easiest to call up.
- **The counter-example (most important):** deliberately collecting five years of claims data and calmly computing the optimal insurance plan is **not** a heuristic — it needs heavy cognitive resources. That's System 2.
- **Corollary:** the direction of availability bias is set by your **exposure structure**, not by the facts. Media is not a neutral probability announcer; it is a **probability distorter** — coverage tracks newsworthiness, not frequency.

> *"The availability heuristic: the brain estimates event probability by retrieval ease; recently and frequently reported information gets unreasonably high weight."* (paraphrased; concept attribution to Tversky & Kahneman — confidence: high)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** I thought I understood availability — "being dragged by the news," so I figured the detection method was asking "is this a trending topic?"
- **After — I got this one wrong.** The question: which daily phenomenon **least** reflects the availability heuristic? Options A (post-crash flight avoidance), B (using 123456 as a password), D (habitually renewing with the old telecom) are all availability; C (collecting five years of claims data and calmly computing the best plan) is not. **I picked B**, reasoning that passwords are "laziness," not probability estimation. The dialogue pushed back: *"That is not quite it."* **My real error:** I narrowed availability to *probability estimation*. It's a whole class of **retrieval substitution** — any time an easy-to-recall answer replaces a hard-to-get one. "123456" substitutes an easily recalled string for the random string security actually requires. Same mechanism.
- **The third layer:** the criterion got corrected. Don't ask "is this a hot topic"; ask: **"Is my answer right, or is it just easy to recall?"** That question works beyond probability — passwords, vendors, plans, names.

```text
Availability self-check (run before any judgment)

  ① Does my number/conclusion come from statistics or from memory?
     → memory → tag "availability risk: high"
  ② How many channels showed me this topic in the last week?
     → high density → discount it, and ask whether those channels
       all cite the same source
  ③ Reverse test: if this had never been reported, would I give the same estimate?
     → no → my estimate is a function of coverage, not of facts
```
