# 06. Prospect Theory: Why Losing ¥100 Takes ¥250 of Winning to Feel Even

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Write the stop-loss as a mechanical order before entry — never rely on in-session willpower.** Every "wait a bit longer" in the loss zone isn't a judgment; it's the reflection effect deciding for you.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **The scene: a coin flip.** Heads you win ¥1000, tails you lose ¥1000. Expected value zero; a rational agent without strong risk aversion should be indifferent. **The overwhelming majority firmly refuse.**
- **Kahneman & Tversky's prospect theory has two core components:**
  - 📉 **Loss aversion** — the pain of a loss far exceeds the pleasure of an equal gain; the source material's figure is **2 to 2.5×** (confidence: medium; the commonly cited estimate is λ ≈ 2.25 — same magnitude).
  - 🎯 **The reflection effect** — in the gain zone we "take the money and run" (risk-averse); in the loss zone we suddenly "go for broke" (risk-seeking), just to avoid realizing a certain loss.
- **Why this runs deeper than "fear of loss":** risk appetite is **not personality — it's a function of position**. The same person is risk-averse in gains and risk-seeking in losses. **You don't need to change character; you only need to move the reference point.**
- **Direct corollary: the disposition effect.** In markets: sell winners fast (gain zone risk aversion), hold losers and even add (loss zone risk seeking).
- **The deeper cause: unrealized isn't lost.** While the position stays open, the loss remains "floating," unbooked. Selling forces the psychological admission "I was wrong" — and its full pain. **Holding is an accounting delay tactic.**

> *"In the market, as long as you don't sell, you can tell yourself 'I haven't really lost yet.' The moment you sell, you must admit it in full and carry the pain."* (paraphrased; confidence: medium)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** I read "holding and hoping" as a technical problem — wrong trend call, oversized position. So the fix was "raise win rate" and "control position size."
- **After:** I answered the quiz correctly — *stock keeps falling, fundamentals deteriorate, investor refuses to realize the loss and keeps averaging down hoping for a miracle* — driven by loss aversion. I thought I had it. **The real turn came later**, when the rules walkthrough reclassified averaging down as **escalation of commitment** — not a trend judgment error, but *continuously investing resources to postpone the psychological pain of admitting the first decision was wrong*. That distinction is decisive: if it's a judgment problem, "better judgment" cures it. If it's a **pain-deferral machine**, only **external mechanisms** cure it.
- **The third layer:** a stop-loss is therefore not a "risk parameter" — it's **forced settlement of the books**. That's also why "treat the stop as cost of goods" is the hardest and most valuable rule: it rewrites the frame from "my judgment failed" to "a necessary cost of doing business." **What changes is the reference point, not the personality.**

```text
Three hard rules for the loss zone (productized; independent of daily state)

  Rule A · Pre-bound
    At the moment the entry order goes out, simultaneously set an automated
    conditional stop-loss
    → manual cancellation mid-session is forbidden. Reason: at cancellation
      time you are in the loss zone (risk-seeking state)

  Rule B · Daily zero-based question
    Before each session: "If I held the equivalent in cash today,
    would I buy this position at the current price?"
    → no → reduce or exit at market immediately
    Reason: holding and buying at current price carry identical opportunity cost

  Rule C · Averaging-down ban
    When a position is underwater, no adds to lower the average price
    outside a pre-planned left-side grid
    → test: "Is this add to lower my cost, or because a planned signal fired?"
```
