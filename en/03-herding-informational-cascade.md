# 03. Herding: Why You Don't Dare Enter the Empty "Hidden Gem" Restaurant

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Write down your independent judgment before you look at anyone else's.** Reversing the order means you never saw the data — after the consensus, your "independent view" is only its echo.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **The scene: two restaurants on an unfamiliar food street.** A is empty; B has a 30-minute queue. Many people stay hungry rather than enter the empty A.
- **Classical economics assumes everyone judges independently.** Under scarcity of information, that's the first assumption to break.
- **Two mechanisms fire at once:**
  - 🐑 **Herding** — we tend to imitate the majority.
  - 🧠 **Informational influence / social learning** — when information is unclear, we treat *other people's choices* as intelligence: "so many people queue, it must be good." The queue produces **no new information**, but it transmits the signal "someone already validated this."
- **The fatal step: the informational cascade.** Once the queue is long enough, latercomers **read the queue and discard their own private signals**. Further down the chain, independent judgment disappears; only one signal remains — "people are ahead of me."
- **The real cost: valuable private information gets buried, and the group walks collectively into second-best.** Note — **queuing isn't necessarily wrong**; crowds often do carry information. What's fatal is the *act of discarding your private signal*: once everyone does it, the market loses its only error-correction mechanism.

> *"When everyone abandons their real private information to follow the crowd, the group walks collectively into the wrong judgment."* (paraphrased; confidence: medium)

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** I treated herding as a courage problem — as if being contrarian enough made me immune. So I assumed I wouldn't make this mistake.
- **After:** the quiz pushed the scenario to the extreme — a friend *privately vouches* that empty Restaurant A is a hidden gem, but on site A is empty and B is queued. The question asked the cost of "ignoring your own private information and following the crowd." I answered correctly — but the real gain was the reframe: **herding isn't "following others," it's "destroying what you're holding."**
- **The third layer:** contrarianism (deliberately choosing the empty shop) is also information destruction — it ignores that the queue may genuinely carry information. The real fix is **fixing your private judgment before contacting the consensus**: write the judgment, then look at the market, then compare. The gap between the two is itself the most valuable signal.

```text
Anti-herding workflow (adapted from how I actually pick targets / vendors / plans)

  Step 1 · Isolate   Before any external opinion, write your judgment + reason
                     → one line is enough, but the "reason" field is mandatory
  Step 2 · Collect   Now look at the consensus (community heat, analyst views, peers)
  Step 3 · Diff      my judgment vs consensus
        ├─ match → zero marginal info; I may just be reciting the consensus
        └─ differ → where? different data, or different interpretation?
  Step 4 · Grade     Does the diff come from private information others lack?
                     → yes → that's the alpha source; verify harder
                     → no, just a hunch → treat as noise; follow consensus or pass
  Step 5 · Reverse   Force out one reason the consensus might be right
                     → can't find one → I haven't understood the consensus,
                       the consensus isn't wrong
```
